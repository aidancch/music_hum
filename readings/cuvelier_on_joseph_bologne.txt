The arts and amity have just lost the celebrated St.-George....This man, the most amazing of his century by the variety and superiority of his talents, had for some time been tormented by a violent fever, that left him for a moment only to assault him once again with renewed fury. His vigorous temperament has often gained the advantage against this cruel illness yet after a month of suffering he succumbed on the 21st of Prairial at five o’clock in the evening. During this last period of time he retired to the home of a friend, in the rue Boucherat, his death was marked by the calm of the wise and the dignity of the man of strong will.

St. George had reached his sixtieth year: born with a fiery temperament and the most outstanding physical resources, he was a constant supporter of the weak and opponent of the oppressor; his urbanity, his gentle manners and benevolent soul always endeared him to those who sought his company; obliging, generous, witty, gallant and sensitive, his loss is lamented equally by the fair sex, enthralled by his gifts, and by his friends who readily forgave the slight aberrations of his head, in favor of the precious qualities of his heart.

The entire world knows St. George’s superiority in the art of fencing; he was no less superior in the dance, equitation, music, and without exception in all the physical exercises. He composed with infinite ease and natural grace: he had left us with a number of operas, that were only kept from achieving grand success by the weakness of their librettos; a multitude of charming romances, and concertos, several of which, though published under the name of the famous Jarnovitch, were by no means disavowed by that great master.

During the last two years of his life he had been particularly devoted to the violin, and he used to say that he had never played it better. Indeed, he was so expert in that field, that if I might dare to use the expression, I would say that he was the VOLTAIRE of the musical arts.

The tender friendship that he had always demonstrated towards me and the affinity of our tastes, empower me to spread a shade over his tomb with the first cypress.... Artists more able than myself might offer his memory the proper eulogy his virtues and talents deserve.... but none shall feel more keenly his loss!

— J. S. A. CUVELIER
LE COURIER DES SPECTACLES
